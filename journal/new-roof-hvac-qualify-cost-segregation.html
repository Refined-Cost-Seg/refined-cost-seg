Does a new roof or a new HVAC system qualify for cost segregation?

Refined Cost SegregationResidential cost segregation

No. On a residential rental, a replacement roof and a new furnace or central air conditioner are building components. They sit on the same 27.5-year schedule as the structure itself, and a cost segregation study does not move them into the 5-, 7-, or 15-year buckets. That surprises plenty of owners who have just written a five-figure check for a roof. The useful part is that the rest of a rent-ready renovation usually does qualify, and on a property you bought this year the study is still where most of the first-year deduction comes from. Here is the line between the two, with a worked example on a $340,000 single-family rental.

Why a roof and HVAC stay on the 27.5-year schedule.

Depreciation life follows what a component is, not what it cost. Anything that is part of the building's structure or its permanently installed operating systems is 27.5-year residential rental property: the roof, the framing, the windows, the siding, the plumbing and electrical rough-in, and the heating and air-conditioning system that serves the building. A cost segregation study is an exercise in identifying property that is genuinely shorter-lived, meaning personal property under the IRS asset classes and land improvements. It is not a tool for relabeling the building.

Because 100% bonus depreciation only reaches property with a recovery period of 20 years or less, a 27.5-year roof is not bonus-eligible either. Putting it on in December buys you a fraction of a year of straight-line depreciation, not a write-off. One narrow exception is worth knowing: a window unit or a portable air conditioner is equipment rather than a building system, and it lands in the 5-year bucket alongside the appliances.

What a study does pull out of a rent-ready renovation.

On a typical 1-4 unit renovation, the items that move are the ones you expect to replace again within a decade: carpet and vinyl plank, appliances, cabinetry and countertops in many configurations, window treatments, specialty electrical serving equipment, and decorative lighting and millwork. Those are 5- or 7-year property. Outside, the driveway, walkways, fencing, retaining walls, and landscaping are 15-year land improvements.

All three buckets are 20 years or less, so with 100% bonus depreciation in effect for property acquired (contract signed) after January 19, 2025, they are fully deductible in the first year the property is in service. The study's job is to put a defensible number on each component and document how it got there. The roof and the furnace stay where they are, and a good report says so plainly, which is part of why the file holds up as audit-ready.

A worked example: a $340,000 rental with $27,500 of rent-ready work.

Say you bought a single-family rental for $340,000, signed the contract in March 2026, and put about $27,500 into it before the first tenant moved in that June: $18,000 for a new roof, $5,000 for a furnace and condenser, and $4,500 for flooring and appliances. The assessor's record supports roughly 20% to land, so $68,000 is land, $272,000 is building, plus the $27,500 of improvements.

Do nothing special and the whole $299,500 rides the 27.5-year schedule. A June placed-in-service date carries a first-year factor of 1.970%, which is about $5,900 of depreciation.

Now run a study. It reclassifies roughly 21% of the $272,000 building basis, about $57,120, into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property, and it separately identifies the $4,500 of flooring and appliances as 5-year. That is $61,620 deductible in year one under 100% bonus. The remaining $237,880, roof and furnace included, stays on 27.5 years and contributes about $4,686. First-year depreciation goes from roughly $5,900 to roughly $66,300. At a 32% marginal federal rate, that is about $19,300 of additional first-year federal tax savings, before any state effect.

Notice what did the work. The roof and the HVAC contributed almost nothing to year one. The study still produced the result, because the components it could legitimately move were sitting inside the purchase price the whole time.

What if you replaced the roof on a rental you were already renting?

Then the question changes shape. You are still not reclassifying the new roof, which remains 27.5-year property. But the roof you tore off has remaining basis sitting on your depreciation schedule, and a partial disposition election on a timely-filed return lets you write that remainder off as a loss in the year of the replacement. On a rental you have held for a few years, that is often a larger first-year number than anything the new roof itself could produce. Our note on the partial disposition election walks through how the old component's basis gets carved out.

Does this change whether you replace the roof before December 31?

Mostly, no. If the roof needs replacing, replace it on the schedule the building dictates. A 27.5-year component placed in service on December 20 produces a few dollars of depreciation for the year, so the calendar is not much of an argument either way. What does turn on December 31 is the short-life work: flooring, appliances, furnishings, and site work that is complete and in service by year-end is fully deductible this year under bonus depreciation, while the same invoice dated in January becomes a next-year deduction. If you are sequencing a renovation across the turn of the year, that is the part worth sequencing, and the placed-in-service date governs it, not the date you paid.
