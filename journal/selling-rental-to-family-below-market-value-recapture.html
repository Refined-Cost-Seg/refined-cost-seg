Selling a rental to family below market value: recapture

Refined Cost SegregationResidential cost segregation

Yes, depreciation recapture still applies. Selling a rental to your child, your sibling, or any other relative at a friendly price is a sale, not a gift, and recapture is owed on every dollar of gain you actually recognize. The discount between what they pay and what the property is worth is treated separately, as a gift — so you can end up filing a Form 709 and writing a check to the IRS in the same transaction.

If a cost segregation study has already accelerated depreciation on the property, the gain that survives the discount is taxed at the least favorable rate available.

Is a sale to a family member below market value still a sale?

For income tax purposes it is split in two. The portion your relative pays for is a sale: you compute gain against your adjusted basis exactly as you would with an arm's-length buyer. The portion you did not charge for is a gift, measured as fair market value minus the price paid.

There is no family discount on the sale half. Recapture under §1245 and §1250 is triggered by the disposition, and who the buyer is does not reduce it. The low price shrinks the gain, which sounds helpful until you look at which gain disappears first.

A worked example: a $480K rental sold to a son for $400,000

Say you bought a single-family rental for $480,000 all in, with 80% treated as depreciable basis after excluding land. That is $384,000.

A residential cost segregation study reclassifies roughly 22% of that basis into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property, so about $84,000 moves out of the 27.5-year bucket. With 100% bonus depreciation available for property acquired (contract signed) and placed in service after January 19, 2025, that $84,000 is deductible in year one. At a 32% marginal federal rate, that is roughly $26,900 in year-one federal tax savings, before any state effect.

Three years on, the property is worth about $560,000 and you sell it to your son for $400,000. Over those three years you also took straight-line depreciation on the remaining $300,000 of 27.5-year basis, call it $32,700. Total depreciation taken is about $116,700, so your adjusted basis is roughly $363,300.

Your gain is $400,000 minus $363,300, or about $36,700. The discount removed most of the gain, and many owners stop there and assume a modest capital gains bill.

Why the gain that survives is taxed at the worst rate

Recapture is not computed after capital gain; it comes first. §1245 recapture on the short-life components a study identified is the lesser of the depreciation taken on them or the gain allocable to them, and it is taxed as ordinary income. With $84,000 of §1245 depreciation behind you and only $36,700 of gain left, essentially the entire gain is ordinary-income recapture.

At a 32% rate, that is roughly $11,700 of federal tax on the sale. The $26,900 you saved in year one stays saved, but none of that $36,700 gets the 25% §1250 rate or the long-term capital gains rate. A discount strips out the cheaply-taxed gain and leaves the expensive gain standing.

What happens to the discount?

The $160,000 you did not charge is a gift. It almost certainly exceeds the annual per-donee exclusion, which is indexed each year, so the excess is typically reported on Form 709 and applied against your lifetime exemption rather than producing a payment. Confirm the current figures with your preparer.

Your son's basis is not the $363,300 that would carry over on an outright gift. On a part-sale, part-gift to a relative, his basis is generally the greater of what he paid or your adjusted basis — here, the $400,000 he paid. He also starts a fresh depreciation schedule on his allocable share, which is often the real reason families structure it this way.

Can I take a loss if I sell to a relative at a discount?

No. §267 disallows a loss on a sale between related parties outright, and unlike most disallowed losses it is not deferred to the buyer — it simply vanishes. If the property is worth less than your adjusted basis, selling it to family is the one structure guaranteed to waste the loss. Accelerated depreciation usually keeps you on the gain side of that line, since a study pushes adjusted basis down fast.

Does an installment sale spread the recapture out?

Not on depreciable property sold to a related party. §453(g) shuts the installment method off for that combination, so the full gain is recognized in the year of sale even if your relative pays you over ten years. Owner-financing a sale to a child buys you no payment plan on the tax — worth knowing before you sign a note.

Does closing in December or January change anything?

It changes which year absorbs the recapture, which gift-year exclusion applies, and when the tax comes due. A sale closing December 31 lands the ordinary-income recapture in this year's return, where it may collide with a high-income year or trigger an estimated payment by January 15. Push it to January and you get a full year to plan around it.

Depreciation for the year of sale splits at closing too: you claim it through the month of disposition, so a late-December closing leaves nearly the full year's deduction with you.

Before you sign anything

Three numbers decide whether this structure works: your adjusted basis, the current fair market value, and how much of your depreciation sits in §1245 short-life property rather than §1250 building. A study report gives you the third in a form your preparer can use directly, which turns the recapture math into something you model rather than discover. If you are weighing a pure gift instead, read whether gifting a rental triggers depreciation recapture — no recapture today, but your relative inherits the position.
