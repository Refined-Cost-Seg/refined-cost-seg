Do driveways and fences qualify for bonus depreciation?

Refined Cost SegregationResidential cost segregation

Short answer: yes. A driveway, a fence, landscaping, a sidewalk, a parking pad, and a retaining wall are not part of the building and they are not land. They are land improvements, which carry a 15-year recovery period, and 15-year property is bonus-depreciation-eligible. With 100% bonus depreciation back in effect for property acquired (contract signed) and placed in service after January 19, 2025, site work like this can be deducted in full in the year it goes in rather than spread across fifteen years.

What actually counts as a 15-year land improvement?

The category is broader than most investors expect. On a typical residential rental it usually includes the driveway and any paved parking area, walkways and sidewalks, fencing and gates, retaining walls, exterior landscaping such as shrubs, trees, and sod, site drainage and culverts, exterior site lighting and the conduit serving it, and outdoor features like a patio slab or a detached deck that is a site feature rather than an extension of the dwelling.

Two things sit outside the category. Raw land is never depreciable, which is why the land allocation on a purchase matters so much. And anything functionally part of the dwelling stays on the 27.5-year schedule, so a replacement roof, exterior siding, or a deck tied structurally into the house is building property, not a land improvement.

Why the 15-year life is the whole point.

Bonus depreciation applies to property with a recovery period of 20 years or less. The residential building itself sits at 27.5 years, so it never qualifies. That single distinction is why classifying site work correctly is worth real money: the same check written to a contractor either becomes an immediate deduction or becomes a trickle, and what decides it is whether the work is documented as a land improvement or quietly absorbed into building basis.

A worked example: $38,000 of site work on a single-family rental.

Say you own a single-family rental and this year you spend $38,000 on site work: $19,000 to tear out and repour the asphalt driveway, $9,500 on perimeter fencing, $6,000 on regrading, drainage, and new sod, and $3,500 on a small retaining wall along the back of the lot. All four items are 15-year land improvements, and all four were contracted and completed after January 19, 2025.

Classified correctly and claimed with 100% bonus, the full $38,000 is deductible this year. At a 32% marginal federal rate that is roughly $12,160 in federal tax savings, before any state benefit.

Now the alternative. Depreciate the same $38,000 over 15 years with no bonus election and the first-year deduction under the half-year convention is 5% of basis, about $1,900, worth roughly $608 at the same rate. Absorb it into the building at 27.5 years instead and it is smaller still. The work is identical; the classification is the entire difference.

Does the work have to be finished before December 31?

It has to be placed in service, which for site work generally means complete and ready for its intended use, not merely paid for or ordered. A driveway poured in November and cured is in service this year. A fence you put a deposit on in December that the crew installs in March is a next-year deduction, and writing the check early does not change that. If you are weighing a project in the last weeks of the year, the question to ask the contractor is not when they can invoice you but when the work will actually be finished. What about site work you bought as part of the purchase price?

This is the part most owners miss. When you buy a property, you do not just buy a building and land; you buy the driveway, the fence, and the mature landscaping that came with it. Those are land improvements with their own 15-year life, bonus-eligible on the same acquired-after-January-19-2025 test as any other used property you buy.

The problem is that your settlement statement says one number. Nothing in the closing paperwork breaks out the driveway. Without an allocation, your tax preparer has two defensible options: put everything into land and the 27.5-year building, or get a study that itemizes it. That is exactly the gap a cost segregation study closes, and it is why the land-improvement bucket is often the second-largest reclassification on a residential study after the five- and seven-year interior components. Our breakdown of what a study reclassifies into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property shows how the buckets typically split.

What to keep in the file.

For work you pay for directly, keep the contractor's invoice itemized by scope, not a single line reading "exterior improvements." Separate asphalt from fencing from plantings. Keep before-and-after photos with dates, the signed contract showing when the work was committed, and a completion date you can point to. If a single contractor did both land-improvement work and work on the dwelling in the same job, ask for the invoice split by scope at the time of billing; reconstructing it two years later during an examination is much harder than getting it right up front.

That documentation is what makes the position audit-ready. The classification itself is well-established; what gets challenged is a number nobody can trace back to a source.

One caution on the deduction itself.

Creating the deduction and using it are two different questions. A large first-year write-off still runs through the passive activity loss rules, so whether it offsets your other income this year depends on material participation, real estate professional status, or the short-term rental rules. The work is worth doing either way, since suspended losses carry forward, but do not plan a tax bill around the deduction before your preparer confirms you can use it.
