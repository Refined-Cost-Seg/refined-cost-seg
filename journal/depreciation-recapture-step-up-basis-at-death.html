Does depreciation recapture disappear at death?

Refined Cost SegregationResidential cost segregation

Yes. If you hold a rental until you die, the depreciation recapture you have been carrying never comes due. Death is not a sale, so nothing is recaptured on the final return, and the property passes to your heirs with a basis stepped up to its fair market value on the date of death under §1014. Every dollar of depreciation you claimed over the years — including the accelerated 5-, 7-, and 15-year deductions a cost segregation study identified — goes with it. The basis reset wipes the slate.

That is the flat answer. The nuance worth knowing is what it means for whether a study was worth running, and the answer there is that a step-up makes the study better, not worse.

Why death does not trigger recapture

Recapture under §1245 and §1250 attaches to a disposition in which you realize gain. Dying is not a disposition. No sale, no exchange, no gain realized, so there is no amount for recapture to attach to and nothing lands on the decedent's final Form 1040.

Then §1014 does the second half of the work. The heir's basis becomes the date-of-death fair market value, not the depreciated basis you were carrying. Because basis resets from the outside rather than being adjusted from the inside, the depreciation history does not travel with the property the way it does with a lifetime gift. The heir starts fresh: new basis, new 27.5-year clock, no inherited recapture exposure.

A worked example: a $620,000 rental held until death

Say you bought a single-family rental for $620,000 all in, with 80% treated as depreciable basis after excluding land. That is $496,000 to depreciate.

A residential study reclassifies roughly 22% of that basis into short-life components, about $109,000. With 100% bonus depreciation available on property acquired (contract signed) and placed in service after January 19, 2025, that $109,000 is deductible in year one. At a 35% marginal federal rate, that is about $38,150 of year-one federal tax savings, before any state effect.

Now hold the property nine more years. Straight-line depreciation on the remaining basis adds roughly $127,000, so you have claimed about $236,000 of depreciation in total and your adjusted basis is down to roughly $384,000.

If you sold at a $780,000 value, that $236,000 of depreciation would be recaptured. The short-life personal-property components, call it $82,000, are §1245 property taxed as ordinary income — about $28,700 at 35%. The land improvements and the building's straight-line depreciation, roughly $154,000 together, become unrecaptured §1250 gain capped at 25% — about $38,500. Call it $67,200 of federal tax on the depreciation alone, before capital gains on the rest of the appreciation.

If instead you hold until death, your heir's basis becomes $780,000. That $67,200 is never paid by anyone. The $38,150 you saved in year one stays saved. The deferral did not become a liability; it became permanent.

How is this different from gifting the property during your life?

Completely different, and it is the distinction that trips people up. A lifetime gift uses carryover basis under §1015: the recipient takes your depreciated basis and inherits your recapture position, so the bill is deferred rather than erased. A transfer at death uses §1014 and resets basis to market value, so the bill is gone. Same family, same property, two opposite tax outcomes depending on timing. If a transfer to family is on the table, read what happens when you gift a rental after a cost segregation study before anything gets recorded.

Does a step-up mean the study was wasted?

No — it means the study paid off twice. The usual argument against accelerating depreciation is that you are borrowing a deduction you will repay at sale. A step-up removes the repayment. You took the deduction at your marginal rate while you were alive and earning, and the recapture it created died with you.

This is why hold-forever investors tend to be the best candidates for a study, not the worst. The owners who need to think hardest about recapture are the ones planning to sell within a few years.

Where the step-up does not apply

A few limits are worth naming plainly. The step-up applies to property includible in the decedent's gross estate, so assets moved out of the estate during life — certain irrevocable trust structures, for example — may not get one. For jointly owned property, how much of the basis steps up depends on how title is held and on whether the property sits in a community property state, where a full step-up on both halves is often available. A step-up can also be a step down: if the property is worth less than your adjusted basis at death, basis resets downward and the built-in loss disappears. And estate tax is its own question, with an exemption amount that is indexed and changes, so confirm the current figure with your preparer rather than assuming.

None of that changes the recapture answer. It changes how much basis the heir gets.

Can heirs run their own cost segregation study?

Yes, and it is usually the right move. The heir's stepped-up basis is a brand-new depreciable basis placed in service when they inherit, which means a fresh study on the new, higher number — frequently a larger deduction than the original owner ever had, because it is measured against today's value rather than a decade-old purchase price. If you are on the receiving end of a property, how a study works on inherited property covers the mechanics.

What to settle before year-end

If you are deciding this year whether to sell a long-held rental or keep it, recapture belongs in the comparison. If the property is likely to stay in the family, a study on a recently acquired rental is still worth running now: the year-one deduction is yours, and a later step-up means you never give it back.
