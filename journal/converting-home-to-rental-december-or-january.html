Converting your home to a rental in December: do it now or wait for January?

Refined Cost SegregationResidential cost segregation

Here is the flat answer, because it is mid-November and you need to decide something. If you bought the house before January 19, 2025, converting it to a rental in December buys you very little for this tax year — on a $420,000 depreciable basis, somewhere around $3,700 of deduction. If you bought it after January 19, 2025, converting by December 31 can be worth tens of thousands this year, because 100% bonus depreciation lands in full in the year you place the property in service. The purchase date decides, not the conversion date.

That is the whole decision. Here is why, and what each case is actually worth.

First, a December conversion only counts if the rental is genuinely placed in service.

Moving out is not placing a property in service. Depreciation starts when the unit is ready and available to rent: the personal items are gone, it is habitable and listed, and you are actually holding it out to tenants or guests. A house you vacate on December 20 and list on January 8 is a January property, whatever the calendar says about when you left. If you want a 2026 placed-in-service date, the listing has to be live and the unit rent-ready by December 31, and you should keep the listing screenshot and the dated photos in the file.

Also worth knowing: the conversion itself sets your depreciable basis at the lesser of your adjusted cost basis or fair market value on the conversion date, with land stripped out — we walk through that rule in converting a home to a rental: your depreciation basis. Everything below assumes that number is settled.

Case one: you have owned the house for years, so there is no bonus depreciation.

For a converted home, "acquired" means the date you originally bought the house. Conversion is not an acquisition, so a house you bought in 2015 is a 2015 acquisition for bonus purposes — which means none of the components a study reclassifies qualify for bonus at all. The full reasoning is in cost segregation and bonus rules on a converted home.

Without bonus, a December placed-in-service date is a weak year. Say the building-only basis is $420,000 and a residential study reclassifies 22% of it, about $92,400, into $55,000 of 5-year personal property and $37,400 of 15-year land improvements, leaving $327,600 on the 27.5-year schedule. Residential buildings use the mid-month convention, so a December date gives the building half a month: $327,600 at 0.152% is about $498. And because the reclassified short-life property is your only such addition for the year and it all lands in the fourth quarter, the mid-quarter convention applies to it, cutting the 5-year slice to 5% ($2,750) and the 15-year slice to 1.25% ($468). Total 2026 deduction: roughly $3,716, worth about $1,190 at a 32% marginal rate.

Convert on January 2 instead and the same property throws off about $11,417 on the building, $11,000 on the 5-year property at the normal half-year convention, and $1,870 on the land improvements — roughly $24,287, about $7,770 of tax at the same rate. (This is the mirror image of the usual December question; see whether the mid-quarter convention hurts a December rental, where 100% bonus normally leaves it nothing to slow.)

Now the part people get wrong in the other direction: converting in December is still not a mistake. Nothing is lost, only shifted. The December path produces about $3,716 in 2026 and roughly $36,500 in 2027, against $24,287 for the January path — more than $40,000 across the two years versus $24,000, because you started the clock a year earlier. The honest framing is that a December conversion is fine and slightly ahead over the hold; it simply will not deliver the big current-year write-off you may be picturing.

Case two: you bought the house after January 19, 2025, and the answer flips completely.

If you bought the home in, say, March 2025 and are converting it now, the acquisition date clears the One Big Beautiful Bill trigger: property acquired (contract signed) and placed in service after January 19, 2025 is eligible for 100% bonus depreciation, and you never held a depreciable interest in the house while you lived in it. The reclassified short-life components can be written off in full in the year of conversion.

Same $420,000 basis, same 22% reclassification: the $55,000 of 5-year property and $37,400 of 15-year land improvements come off in full, plus the building's $498 of mid-month depreciation. That is about $92,898 of 2026 deduction, roughly $29,700 in cash at a 32% rate. Bonus depreciation is not reduced by the mid-quarter or mid-month conventions, so the December date costs you almost nothing here. Wait until January and that entire $92,400 write-off moves to the 2027 return.

So in case two, December 31 is a real deadline with a real number attached, and the thing standing between you and it is usually not the tax rule — it is whether the unit is actually rent-ready and listed in time.

What to do with this before the end of the year.

Find your original purchase date first; it answers the question faster than any calculator. If it is after January 19, 2025 and you can get the property genuinely listed and rent-ready by December 31, a December conversion is usually worth pushing for, and a study engaged now can land on this year's return. If you bought earlier, take the pressure off: convert when it makes sense for the property, and expect the deduction to build over the following years rather than arrive in one lump. You can sanity-check your numbers with the savings estimate on our homepage.

One caveat on both cases: if your rental losses are passive and you have no offsetting income, a large year-one deduction may be suspended rather than usable. Worth raising with your CPA before you rush a listing live on December 29.
