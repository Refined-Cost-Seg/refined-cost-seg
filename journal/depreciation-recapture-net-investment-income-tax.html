Does depreciation recapture trigger the 3.8% NIIT?

Refined Cost SegregationResidential cost segregation

Yes, in most cases. If you sell a rental you held as a passive investment, the depreciation recapture portion of your gain is net investment income, so the 3.8% net investment income tax applies on top of whatever rate the recapture itself carries — once your modified AGI clears $200,000 single or $250,000 married filing jointly. There are two real exits, both narrower than most sellers assume.

Why recapture lands inside net investment income.

The 3.8% surtax applies to the lesser of your net investment income or the amount your modified AGI exceeds the threshold. Those thresholds — $200,000 for single filers, $250,000 for joint filers, $125,000 for married filing separately — are statutory and have never been indexed for inflation, which is why more sellers cross them every year.

Gain from selling property held in a passive activity counts as net investment income. Recapture is not carved out. It doesn't matter that Section 1245 recapture is taxed at ordinary rates, or that unrecaptured Section 1250 gain has its own 25% ceiling. Character affects the rate, not whether the gain is investment income. For a landlord who collects rent and doesn't materially participate, all of it rides inside the 3.8% base.

Which slices of the gain get hit.

A rental sale after a study usually produces three slices, and the surtax reaches all three for a passive owner. The personal-property slice — appliances, carpet, cabinetry, the 5- and 7-year assets a study identifies — comes back as Section 1245 ordinary recapture, capped at the depreciation actually claimed. The building slice comes back as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at a maximum 25% rate. Anything above original cost is Section 1231 gain at long-term capital rates. We walk through how those slices get sorted in is depreciation recapture ordinary income or capital gains.

One trap worth naming: an installment sale does not spread the recapture. Section 1245 recapture income is recognized in full in the year of disposition even when the rest of the gain is reported over time, so the 3.8% on that slice lands in year one along with it.

What the 3.8% actually costs: a worked example.

Take a $640,000 single-family rental with 80% of the price treated as depreciable basis after carving out land — $512,000. A reasonable residential study moves about 22% of that into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property, so roughly $112,000 leaves the 27.5-year schedule. With 100% bonus depreciation in effect for property acquired (contract signed) after January 19, 2025, that full $112,000 is deductible in year one. At a 35% marginal federal rate, that's about $39,200 of year-one federal tax savings.

Now sell in year six at a gain large enough to recapture all of it. That $112,000 comes back as Section 1245 ordinary income: $39,200 at 35%, plus $4,256 of NIIT at 3.8%. So the surtax adds about four thousand dollars to the exit bill — worth knowing before the January 15 estimated payment; see do I owe an estimated tax payment after selling a rental.

A cost segregation study does not create the 3.8%.

This is the part that gets argued badly. The surtax is rate-neutral between the two paths. Run the same $112,000 of depreciation through both:

With the study, it's Section 1245 ordinary recapture — 35% plus 3.8%, so $43,456. Without the study, the same depreciation claimed on the 27.5-year schedule comes back as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain — 25% plus 3.8%, so $32,256. The gap is $11,200, and it is entirely the ten-point rate differential. The 3.8% is identical on both lines. Whatever the study did or didn't do, the surtax was coming.

What's left is a time-value question: five years' use of $39,200 against an $11,200 rate differential at exit. At a 6% discount rate the deferral is worth roughly $12,400, so a five-year hold is near a coin flip and a ten-year hold isn't close. Short holds are where the math turns, as in the three-year case.

Who actually gets out of it?

Two exits, and both are narrower than the seminar version. The first is real estate professional status: if your rental activity is a non-passive trade or business, a safe harbor in the Section 1411 regulations can put the gain outside net investment income. It turns on hours and documentation, not on intent, and it has to hold for the year of sale.

The second is a short-term rental you materially participate in, where the activity may rise to a trade or business for surtax purposes. Material participation under the passive-loss rules and trade-or-business status under the surtax rules are two separate tests, and clearing the first does not automatically clear the second. That's a preparer call on your specific facts, not something to assume.

What does reliably help: suspended passive losses freed on a fully taxable disposition are deductions properly allocable to investment income, so they reduce the surtax base along with your regular taxable income.

The MAGI trap nobody plans for.

The surtax is capped at your MAGI over the threshold, which means the gain does double duty. A large recapture year inflates MAGI, and that can pull interest, dividends, and portfolio capital gains that sat below the line in a normal year into the 3.8% base alongside the rental gain. Sellers who model only the recapture slice routinely understate the bill. If the sale is flexible, splitting it across two tax years is worth modeling before you commit to a closing date.

What to do before December 31.

If a sale is scheduled this year, get the recapture and surtax figures to your preparer now rather than in April — underpayment interest on a six-figure gain is a real number.
