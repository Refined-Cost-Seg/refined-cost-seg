Selling a rental at a loss after a cost segregation study: do you still owe depreciation recapture?

Refined Cost SegregationResidential cost segregation

Short answer: yes, usually. Selling a rental for less than you paid for it does not cancel depreciation recapture. Recapture is measured against your adjusted basis — what the property has left on the books after depreciation — not against your purchase price. A cost segregation study pulls adjusted basis down fast in year one, so a sale that feels like a loss on the closing statement can still be a taxable gain on the return. There is one clean exception, and the line is easy to find once you know what to compare.

Can you owe recapture on a rental you sold below what you paid?

Yes, whenever "loss" means below purchase price but above adjusted basis. The IRS never compares the sale to what you paid. Gain is amount realized minus adjusted basis, and depreciation reduces basis dollar for dollar, whether or not you felt the benefit in the year you claimed it. Every dollar of depreciation you took is a dollar of potential gain waiting at the closing table.

The rule that limits it is just as simple: recapture can never exceed your gain. Sell for less than adjusted basis and there is no gain, so there is no recapture at all — there is a deductible loss instead. Everything below turns on which side of adjusted basis the sale lands on.

Why a cost segregation study makes a loss sale more likely to be taxable

A study reclassifies part of your building basis into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property: appliances, flooring, cabinetry, dedicated electrical, site improvements. Under the current 100% bonus depreciation rules for property acquired (contract signed) after January 19, 2025, that entire slice can be deducted in the first year. Your adjusted basis drops by the full amount immediately rather than over three decades.

That creates a gap. Two years on, the market may have taken your property down 6%, while depreciation has taken your basis down 23%. The sale is an economic loss and a tax gain at the same time, and the character of that gain is the unwelcome part: to the extent it is attributable to depreciation taken on the reclassified personal property, it comes back as Section 1245 recapture — ordinary income at your marginal rate, with none of the 25% cap that applies to the building's unrecaptured Section 1250 gain.

The math on a $480,000 rental sold for $450,000

A single-family rental bought for $480,000 in early 2025, land allocated at $80,000, leaving $400,000 of depreciable building basis. The study moved $88,000 into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property, all of it deducted in 2025 under 100% bonus. Straight-line depreciation on the remaining $312,000 of building added roughly $20,800 across 2025 and 2026.

Total depreciation taken: about $108,800. Adjusted basis: $480,000 − $108,800 = $371,200.

The owner sells in late 2026 for $450,000 and nets $423,000 after commissions and closing costs. Against the $480,000 purchase price that is a $57,000 economic loss. Against adjusted basis it is a $51,800 taxable gain.

Say $30,000 of that gain is allocable to the reclassified personal property, which still carries real resale value. For a seller in the 32% bracket:

$30,000 of Section 1245 recapture × 32% = about $9,600
$20,800 of unrecaptured Section 1250 gain × 25% = about $5,200
$1,000 of remaining Section 1231 gain × 15% = about $150

Roughly $14,950 of federal tax on a sale the owner described as a loss. State tax is separate, and the exact split between ordinary and capital depends on how the sale price is allocated across asset classes.

Was the study still worth it?

Run the whole cycle rather than the exit alone. That $108,800 of depreciation sheltered ordinary income at 32%, worth about $34,800 in tax saved across 2025 and 2026, with the large majority of it landing in the first year. Paying roughly $14,950 back at sale leaves this owner about $19,900 ahead in federal tax, before counting two years of having that money instead of the Treasury.

The spread exists because the property genuinely lost value. You only repay depreciation that the market did not take from you first. That is also why a sale at a loss is usually a better recapture outcome than a sale at a gain, not a worse one — the economic loss absorbs part of the deduction permanently. The broader mechanics of how the categories stack are in our piece on depreciation recapture when you sell after a cost segregation study.

When a sale is genuinely recapture-free

Drop the net sale price below adjusted basis and recapture disappears entirely. Same property, same $371,200 of adjusted basis, but it nets $360,000: that is an $11,200 loss and zero recapture. Better still, a rental held more than a year is Section 1231 property, so the loss is an ordinary loss deductible against ordinary income — not a capital loss capped at $3,000 a year.

One thing usually travels with that outcome: a fully taxable sale to an unrelated party releases any suspended passive losses the property has accumulated, which can be the larger number on the return.

What to settle before you sign the closing statement

Three numbers decide this, and all three exist before closing: your adjusted basis by asset class, the net proceeds after costs, and the allocation of the sale price across the building and the personal property. Your cost segregation report already carries the first one broken out the way your CPA needs it, which is what keeps the recapture figure defensible and the return audit-ready instead of estimated after the fact. The allocation is the piece most sellers never negotiate, and it is where ordinary income and capital gain are decided.

If you are weighing a study on a property you may not hold long, the instant savings estimate gives the order of magnitude on the front end. Then ask your CPA to model the exit beside it, so the deduction and the eventual recapture are on the same page before you commit to either.
